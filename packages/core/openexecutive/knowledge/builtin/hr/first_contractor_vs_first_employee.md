# First Contractor vs. First Employee

For a solo founder, the first person you bring in is a bigger decision than any later hire. It changes your costs, your legal obligations and how you spend your own time. This document covers when to get help, whether that help should be a contractor or an employee, and the risks of getting the classification wrong.

*Employment and tax rules vary by country and region. This is general guidance, not legal or tax advice; confirm the rules where you and the worker are based.*

## When to Get Help at All

Bring someone in when one of these is true:

- Your time is the constraint on revenue — you are turning away work or cannot follow up leads.
- Delivery quality or response times are slipping because you are stretched.
- Recurring tasks outside your strengths (bookkeeping, admin, design, specialist technical work) are taking hours you could spend on higher-value work.

Before hiring, check the cash: the business should be able to cover the cost for several months from current revenue or reserves without relying on work you have not yet won. A useful test is whether the work you would hand off frees enough of your time to earn more than the person costs.

## Contractor or Employee?

### A contractor is usually the better first step when

- The work is a defined project or a variable amount of work each month.
- You need specialist skills you do not need full-time.
- You are not yet sure the need will last.
- The person has other clients, uses their own tools and decides how to do the work.

Benefits: flexibility, no long-term commitment, lower administrative overhead, and a fast way to test whether handing off the work actually helps.

### An employee is usually the better choice when

- The work is ongoing, core to the business, and needs to be done your way, on your schedule.
- You want to train someone, build their skills, and keep them long-term.
- The role involves representing the business to customers day to day.
- You need to direct how, when and where the work is done.

Employment brings obligations that vary by location — payroll taxes, statutory benefits, paid leave, workers' insurance, employment contracts and termination rules — but it also gives you commitment and control that a contractor relationship cannot.

## Classification Risk

Whether someone is a contractor or an employee is decided by the real working relationship, not by what the contract calls them. Authorities commonly look at factors such as:

- **Control** — do you decide how, when and where the work is done, or only the result?
- **Integration** — is the person doing core, ongoing work alongside you, like a staff member?
- **Exclusivity and dependence** — do they work only for you, full-time, with no other clients?
- **Financial risk and tools** — do they invest in their own equipment, set their own rates, and bear the risk of loss?
- **Duration** — is it open-ended rather than a defined engagement?

The more of these point toward employment, the higher the risk. Misclassification can lead to back payroll taxes, penalties, unpaid benefits and claims from the worker. Some jurisdictions apply strict tests that make it hard to treat anyone doing core work as a contractor.

Warning signs that a contractor relationship has drifted into employment:

- They work set hours you define, full-time, for you alone.
- You manage their daily tasks and methods.
- They have a company email, title and role indistinguishable from staff.
- The engagement has no end and no defined deliverables.

If you see these, get local advice and either restructure the relationship or convert the person to an employee.

## Practical Options Between the Two

- **Part-time employee** — a smaller commitment with proper employment status.
- **Employer of record** or payroll services — useful when hiring in another country or when you want to outsource payroll compliance.
- **Fixed-term contract** — where local law allows, for a role with a known end date.
- **Agencies and freelancer platforms** — can reduce administrative load, but check who is responsible for classification and IP.

## Getting the First Relationship Right

1. Write down the outcomes you need and the hours or budget available before looking for anyone.
2. Start with a small paid trial project to test fit and quality.
3. Use a written agreement from day one, including IP assignment and confidentiality (see the contractor agreements guidance).
4. Document the process you are handing off, so the handover does not depend on your memory.
5. Review after a few months: did it free your time, and did that time produce more revenue or better delivery? If yes, consider increasing the commitment; if the work has become ongoing and core, revisit the contractor-versus-employee question.
