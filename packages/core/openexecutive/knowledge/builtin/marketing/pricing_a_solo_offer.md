# Pricing a Solo Offer

For a solo founder or very small team, price is the most powerful lever in the business. Capacity is fixed — there are only so many hours — so revenue grows mainly by charging more per unit of work or by packaging work so it is not tied to hours at all. This document covers the three common models, how to choose between them, and how to raise prices.

## Three Pricing Models

### Hourly or Daily Rate

You bill for time spent.

- **Works well when** scope is genuinely unknown, the client wants to direct the work closely, or you are early and still learning what the work involves.
- **Drawbacks:** income is capped by hours; getting faster earns you less; clients scrutinise time rather than results; and every estimate becomes a negotiation.
- **If you bill hourly:** set a minimum engagement, bill in blocks, and track actual hours against estimates so you learn what work really costs.

### Productized Service (Fixed Scope, Fixed Price)

You sell a defined package — a set deliverable, a timeline and a price — the same way each time. Examples: a website audit, a monthly bookkeeping package, a brand identity sprint.

- **Works well when** you have done similar work enough times to know the effort and the common variations.
- **Benefits:** easy for buyers to understand and approve; sales conversations get shorter; you keep the upside when you get more efficient; delivery can be systematised and eventually delegated.
- **Keys to making it work:** a tight definition of what is included, a clear list of what is not, a limit on revisions, and a separate price for add-ons. Without those, a fixed price becomes unlimited work.

### Value-Based Pricing

You price according to the value of the outcome to the client rather than the effort to deliver it.

- **Works well when** the outcome is significant and measurable for the buyer (revenue gained, costs removed, risk reduced) and you have evidence you can deliver it.
- **How to approach it:** in discovery, establish what the problem costs them and what solving it is worth. Price as a fraction of that value, leaving the client a clear return. Present options at different levels of scope and outcome.
- **Drawbacks:** it requires good discovery and confidence; it is harder with buyers who compare you to hourly competitors; and it does not suit small or routine work.

## Choosing a Model

A practical sequence many independents follow:

1. Start hourly while you learn what the work involves and what clients value.
2. Once you see the same requests repeatedly, package them into fixed-scope offers.
3. For larger engagements where the outcome is measurable, move toward value-based proposals.

You can run more than one model at once: a productized entry offer, a retainer for ongoing work, and value-based pricing for bigger projects.

## Retainers

A monthly retainer trades a guaranteed fee for guaranteed availability or a defined amount of work. Retainers smooth cash flow, which matters more to a solo business than to a funded one. Define what the retainer covers, how unused capacity is handled (it usually does not roll over indefinitely), and how extra work is billed.

## Signs You Are Underpriced

These are heuristics, not proof:

- Almost every proposal is accepted without negotiation.
- You are fully booked but cash is still tight.
- Clients describe you as a bargain.
- You avoid taking on work you would enjoy because it would not pay enough to justify the time.
- Your effective hourly income (revenue divided by all hours worked, including sales and admin) is well below what you would accept as a salary.

A close rate that is very high is usually a sign that price is below what the market will bear.

## Raising Prices

- **New clients first.** Quote the new price to all new prospects; it carries no relationship risk and tells you quickly whether the market accepts it.
- **Existing clients with notice.** Give clear notice (for example, a month or two before the next billing period or renewal), explain briefly, and optionally offer a period at the old rate or a longer commitment at a smaller increase.
- **Tie increases to value where you can.** Additional deliverables, faster turnaround or better results make an increase easier to accept, but you do not need to justify inflation-level increases at length.
- **Expect some churn.** Losing a small number of the most price-sensitive clients after an increase is normal, and often frees time for better-paying work. Model the numbers: a higher price with fewer clients can produce the same or more revenue with less work.
- **Raise regularly.** Small, predictable increases are easier for clients than rare large ones.

## Presenting the Price

- Talk about the outcome and the scope before the number.
- Offer two or three options so the conversation is about which one, not whether.
- State the price plainly and stop talking; do not pre-emptively discount.
- Put payment terms in the proposal — deposits and milestone billing protect cash.
