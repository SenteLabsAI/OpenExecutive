# Contractor Agreements and IP Assignment for a Solo Founder

*Note: This document provides general educational information. It is not legal advice. Contract and IP law differ significantly by country, state and province, and the right terms depend on your situation. Have a licensed attorney in your jurisdiction review your agreements.*

Solo founders rely on contractors — developers, designers, writers, specialists — long before hiring employees. Each of those relationships creates work the business will depend on. If the paperwork does not make the business the owner of that work, the business may not own its own product, brand or content. This is easy to fix at the start and expensive to fix later, often in the middle of a sale, an investment or a dispute.

## The Core Problem: Who Owns the Work

In many jurisdictions, the default rule is that the person who creates a work owns the copyright in it unless a written agreement says otherwise. Paying for the work does not by itself transfer ownership. Without a written assignment, a contractor may own the code, designs or text you paid for, and you may only have an implied permission to use it.

## Work-for-Hire vs. Assignment

**Work made for hire** (a US concept; other countries have different or no equivalents) means the commissioning party is treated as the author from the start. For independent contractors in the US it applies only to certain categories of work and only with a written agreement — and software, for example, may not fit those categories. Relying on a work-for-hire clause alone can leave gaps.

**Assignment** means the contractor owns the work when it is created and transfers ownership to the business under the agreement. A present-tense assignment ("hereby assigns") of all rights in the deliverables is the usual belt-and-braces approach, often combined with a work-for-hire clause where it applies.

Common clauses in a well-drafted contractor agreement:

- **Assignment of IP** in all deliverables and work product created for the business, including drafts and source files.
- **Moral rights waiver or consent**, where local law gives creators rights that cannot be assigned.
- **Further assurances** — the contractor will sign documents needed to register or confirm your ownership.
- **Pre-existing materials** — the contractor keeps ownership of tools and code they created before the engagement, but grants you a perpetual, broad license to use anything incorporated into your deliverables.
- **Third-party and open-source components** — the contractor discloses them and does not include anything under terms incompatible with your use.

## Confidentiality

A confidentiality clause (or a separate NDA) should define what is confidential — customer data, business plans, source code, pricing — require the contractor to protect it, limit use to the engagement, and require return or deletion at the end. If contractors handle personal data about your customers, you may also need data-protection terms that meet the privacy law that applies to you.

## Other Terms Worth Having

- **Scope and deliverables**, with acceptance criteria.
- **Payment terms**, including what happens if the engagement ends early.
- **Independent contractor status** — they control how the work is done, supply their own tools, and are responsible for their own taxes. The label alone does not decide classification (see the note below).
- **Non-solicitation** of your clients or staff, where enforceable. Broad non-compete clauses are unenforceable or restricted in many places.
- **Termination** and the handover of work in progress.
- **Governing law and dispute resolution.**

## Practical Checklist

1. Use a written agreement for every contractor, signed before work starts.
2. Make sure it includes a present-tense assignment of IP, not just a promise to assign later.
3. Collect source files and credentials as part of delivery, not after a dispute.
4. Keep signed copies organised — a buyer or investor will ask for them.
5. If past contractors worked without an agreement, ask them to sign a confirmatory assignment now, while the relationship is good. Paying a small fee for it is common.
6. For your own pre-company work, assign it to the business when you form the company.

## Classification Is a Separate Question

Calling someone a contractor does not make them one. Tax and labour authorities look at the real relationship — control over how work is done, integration into the business, exclusivity, and who bears financial risk. Misclassification can create liability for back taxes, benefits and penalties. See the first-contractor-versus-first-employee guidance and get local advice before a contractor relationship starts to look like employment.

## When to Call a Lawyer

- Before relying on a template for work central to your product.
- When a contractor refuses to assign IP or wants to retain rights.
- Before a fundraise, sale, or major partnership — clean IP ownership is checked in diligence.
- When working with contractors in another country, where the default rules may differ from yours.
