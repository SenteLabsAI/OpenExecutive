# Proposals, Statements of Work and Pricing Conversations

A proposal should confirm a decision the buyer is already close to making, not start the conversation. If the first time a buyer hears the price is in a PDF, the proposal is doing discovery's job, and it will usually be met with silence.

## Before Writing the Proposal

Do not send a proposal until you can state:
- The problem and what it costs the buyer, in their words.
- The outcome they want and how they will judge success.
- Who approves the spend and what their process is.
- A price range the buyer has already heard and not rejected.

Test the price out loud first: "Projects like this typically land between X and Y, depending on scope. Is that in the range you expected?" Their reaction tells you whether to adjust scope, reframe value, or requalify.

## Structure of a Good Proposal

1. **Their situation** — a short restatement of the problem and why it matters now. If they recognise their own words, you have their attention.
2. **The outcome** — what will be different when this is done, with the success measures agreed in discovery.
3. **The approach** — how you will deliver it, in phases with milestones.
4. **Options** — usually two or three packages at different scope and price levels (see below).
5. **Investment and terms** — price, payment schedule, what is included and excluded.
6. **Next step** — how to accept and when work would start.

Keep it short. A long proposal is rarely read in full; the decision-maker often reads only the summary and the price.

## Statements of Work

For services or implementation work, the statement of work (SOW) is where profit is protected or lost. It should define:

- **Deliverables** — concrete, checkable outputs, not activities.
- **Assumptions and client responsibilities** — access, data, approvals and response times you depend on.
- **Out of scope** — the things the buyer might reasonably assume are included but are not.
- **Acceptance** — how a deliverable is accepted, and what happens if feedback does not arrive within a set period.
- **Change process** — how new requests are priced and approved. Scope creep is managed here, not in an awkward conversation later.
- **Payment milestones** — tie payments to dates or deliverables, and take a deposit before work begins where the relationship allows it.

Have contract terms reviewed by a qualified lawyer; the SOW describes the work, the agreement governs liability and IP.

## Offering Options

Presenting options changes the buyer's question from "yes or no?" to "which one?".

- Build the middle option around what discovery says they need; that is the one most buyers choose.
- Make the higher option genuinely more valuable (faster, more support, broader scope), not a padded version of the middle one.
- The lower option should remove scope, not just reduce price. It protects your margin and gives a buyer with a tight budget a real path.

## Discounting Discipline

Discounts are the fastest way to lose margin you will never recover, because the next renewal and the buyer's friends will anchor on the discounted price.

- **Trade, never give.** Every concession gets something back: a longer commitment, upfront payment, a case study, a reference call, a faster signature, or reduced scope.
- **Decide your walk-away point before the call.** Know the lowest price and the terms you will accept, and the point at which you would rather lose the deal.
- **Reduce scope before reducing price.** "We can hit that number if we remove X" keeps your rate intact and tests whether X mattered.
- **Put an expiry on any concession.** An open-ended discount becomes the new list price.
- **Be wary of end-of-quarter pressure.** Buyers learn when sellers are desperate. A company that discounts every quarter-end trains its customers to wait for it.

## Handling "It's Too Expensive"

Treat the objection as a question you do not yet understand. Ask what they are comparing the price to — a competitor, doing nothing, their budget, or what they assumed it would cost. Each has a different answer:

- **Compared to a competitor:** clarify the difference in outcome, risk or effort, or narrow scope to match.
- **Compared to doing nothing:** return to what the problem costs them each month it is not solved.
- **Compared to budget:** offer a smaller first phase or a payment schedule rather than a lower rate.
- **Compared to their assumption:** you skipped price-testing in discovery; restate the value and the options.

If the buyer cannot connect the price to a result they care about, the issue is usually value or fit, not the number.

## Procurement and Paperwork

Larger buyers route purchases through procurement and legal, which can add weeks. Ask early who else must sign and what their process requires (vendor forms, security questionnaires, insurance certificates, redlines). Put that timeline into your forecast; a verbal yes is not a signed contract.
