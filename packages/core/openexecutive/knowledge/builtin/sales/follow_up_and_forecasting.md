# Follow-Up Cadence and Sales Forecasting

Most small deals are not lost to a competitor; they are lost to silence. The buyer got busy, the champion changed priorities, or nobody set the next step. Consistent follow-up and an honest forecast are the two habits that turn a pipeline into predictable revenue.

## Follow-Up Principles

- **Every touch adds something.** A relevant question, a short case example, an answer to an objection they raised, a draft plan. "Just checking in" gives the buyer nothing to respond to.
- **Agree the next step in the meeting.** Follow-up is easiest when the buyer has already said what happens next and when.
- **Use the buyer's channel.** If they reply on email, stay on email; if they text or use chat, follow them there.
- **Write it down the same day.** A short recap after each call — what we heard, what was agreed, who does what by when — keeps the deal moving and surfaces misunderstandings early.

## A Simple Cadence for a Stalled Deal

Adjust the spacing to your sales cycle; the shape is what matters.

1. **Shortly after the missed step:** a short note restating the agreed next step and offering two specific times.
2. **About a week later:** something useful — an example relevant to their problem, or an answer to a question they raised.
3. **A week or two after that:** a direct question about status: "Has this dropped down the priority list? That's fine to say."
4. **Then a close-the-loop note:** "I'll assume the timing isn't right and close this out on my side. If that changes, reply here and we'll pick it up." These often get a reply because they ask for a simple decision.

After that, move the deal to closed lost with a reason, and put the contact on a longer-term nurture list (a check-in every few months, or when you have something genuinely relevant).

## Forecasting Categories

A forecast is a statement of what you expect to close in a period and how confident you are. Use categories based on evidence:

- **Commit** — the buyer has confirmed intent, the economic buyer has approved, and only paperwork remains. You would be surprised to lose it.
- **Best case** — qualified and progressing, with a credible path to closing this period, but a material step remains (approval, budget, procurement).
- **Pipeline** — qualified but unlikely to close this period.

Forecast the commit number as your plan, and treat best case as upside. Do not count pipeline toward the period.

## Weighted Pipeline — Use With Care

Weighted pipeline multiplies each deal's value by a probability tied to its stage. It is useful across many deals and misleading across few: with a handful of opportunities, each deal either closes or does not, and a weighted total describes an outcome that cannot happen. For a small company, a deal-by-deal review of commit and best case is usually more accurate than a weighted sum.

If you do use stage probabilities, derive them from your own history of how often deals at each stage eventually closed, not from a template.

## Checking Forecast Accuracy

At the end of each period compare what you committed to what closed. Look for patterns:

- **Consistent over-forecasting** usually means deals are being advanced without meeting exit criteria, or the economic buyer is not involved.
- **Frequent slipped close dates** usually mean procurement, legal or approval steps were not mapped during discovery.
- **Under-forecasting** is less common and often means small deals close without being tracked — a pipeline hygiene problem.

## Win/Loss Reviews

Review every significant closed deal, won or lost, while memory is fresh. Ask the buyer when possible — lost buyers are often willing to explain briefly if asked politely.

Record: the trigger that started the process, who decided, what alternatives they considered, the deciding factor, and what nearly stopped the deal. Over time these reviews show which customer profiles you win, which objections you cannot yet answer, and whether you are losing on price, product or process — and they are the best input for improving both the product and the pitch.

## Cash Is the Last Stage

For a small company a signed contract is not the finish line; collected cash is. Agree invoicing and payment terms before signature, invoice promptly, and track overdue receivables with the same discipline as open deals. A deal that closes on long payment terms can still leave the business short of cash.
