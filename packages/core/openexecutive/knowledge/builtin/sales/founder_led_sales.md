# Founder-Led Sales

In an early company the founder is the sales team. That is not a stopgap to escape as quickly as possible; it is where the company learns who buys, why they buy, what they will pay, and what the product must do to keep them. A salesperson hired before those answers exist inherits guesswork and is usually blamed for it.

## Why the Founder Sells First

- **Learning, not just revenue.** Every call is customer research. The founder hears objections first-hand and can change the product, the pricing, or the pitch the same week. A hired rep reports the objection; a founder acts on it.
- **Credibility.** Early buyers are betting on the company, not just the product. Talking to the person who will stand behind it lowers their risk.
- **Authority to decide.** The founder can agree a pilot, adjust scope, or walk away without an approval chain. Speed wins early deals.
- **The playbook does not exist yet.** You cannot hand over a process nobody has written down, and you cannot write it down until you have run it enough times to see the pattern.

## The Weekly Rhythm

Selling competes with building, and building usually wins unless selling has protected time. A workable rhythm for a founder who also delivers or builds:

- **Fixed selling blocks.** Put prospecting, follow-ups and calls on the calendar as recurring blocks (for example two or three half-days a week). Treat them like client meetings.
- **One pipeline, reviewed weekly.** A single list of every live opportunity with its stage, next step and next-step date. Thirty minutes a week to clean it is enough at this size.
- **A short learning log.** After each call, note the trigger that made them take the call, the words they used for their problem, the objection, and what happened next. Patterns in this log become the pitch, the qualification questions and the website copy.

## Running a Discovery Call

Discovery decides whether there is a deal at all. Aim to talk less than the buyer and leave with facts, not impressions.

1. **Confirm why now.** What changed that made this worth a meeting this month? No trigger usually means no urgency, and no urgency usually means no deal.
2. **Understand the problem in their words.** What happens today, what it costs them (time, money, risk, reputation), and who feels the pain most.
3. **Map the decision.** Who else is involved, who signs, what the approval steps are, and whether budget exists or must be found.
4. **Surface the alternatives.** Doing nothing, building it themselves, hiring someone, or a competitor. You are always competing with at least one of these.
5. **Agree a concrete next step before hanging up.** A date, an attendee list and what each side will bring. "I'll send some info over" is not a next step.

A useful heuristic: if you cannot write two sentences describing the buyer's problem, its cost and their deadline after the call, discovery is not finished.

## Demos and Pilots

- Demo against the problem they described, not the feature list. Open by restating their situation and show only what addresses it.
- A pilot needs success criteria agreed in writing before it starts, a fixed end date, and a named decision-maker who will review the result. An open-ended free pilot is a way for a buyer to postpone deciding.
- Prefer a paid pilot or a small first engagement over a free one when you can. Payment tests intent in a way enthusiasm does not.

## Knowing the Motion Is Repeatable

Founder-led sales has done its job when you can describe, from evidence rather than hope:

- The customer profile that buys fastest and stays longest.
- The two or three triggers that start a buying process.
- The questions that separate real opportunities from polite interest.
- The objections you hear repeatedly, and answers that work.
- A typical sales cycle length and deal size for that profile.
- A proposal or order form you reuse with light edits.

If most wins still depend on the founder's personal network or on one-off heroics, the motion is not yet repeatable.

## Handing Off

Hire the first salesperson when the founder's time is the constraint on revenue and the playbook above is written down. Common heuristics:

- Hire someone who can operate with an incomplete playbook and enjoys refining it; a rep who has only worked inside a mature sales machine often struggles without one.
- Many founders hire two early reps rather than one, so that one person's results are not mistaken for the viability of the whole motion. Whether that is affordable depends on cash, not on convention.
- Stay on key deals and on every lost-deal review for the first months. The founder's job shifts from closing to coaching and to feeding what sales learns back into the product.

## Where Sales Stops and Marketing Starts

Sales owns converting interest into revenue: qualification, discovery, proposals, negotiation and the forecast. Generating that interest at scale — positioning, content, campaigns, channels and brand — is demand generation, and belongs with marketing. When the pipeline is empty rather than stuck, the problem is usually upstream of sales: fix positioning and lead flow before adding more follow-up activity.
