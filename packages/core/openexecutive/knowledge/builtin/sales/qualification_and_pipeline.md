# Qualification and Pipeline Management

A pipeline is only useful if every entry in it means the same thing. Most small-company pipelines are inflated: they list everyone who took a call, so the total looks healthy while revenue stays flat. The fix is explicit stage definitions, honest qualification, and a weekly habit of removing what is not real.

## Define Stages by What the Buyer Has Done

Stages should describe verifiable buyer actions, not the seller's optimism. A simple set that works for most small B2B sales:

| Stage | Exit criteria (what must be true to move on) |
|---|---|
| **Lead** | A person at a plausible target company has shown interest or replied. |
| **Qualified** | Discovery done: a real problem, a reason to act now, a known decision process, and budget that exists or can be found. |
| **Solution / proposal** | The buyer has seen a tailored proposal or demo and agreed it addresses the problem. |
| **Negotiation / commit** | The buyer has said they intend to buy; terms, paperwork or procurement are what remain. |
| **Closed won / closed lost** | Signed, or explicitly dead with a recorded reason. |

Rules of thumb:
- Fewer stages are better at small scale. Each stage must change what you do next.
- A deal moves forward only when its exit criteria are met, never because time has passed.
- Every open deal carries a next step and a next-step date. A deal with no dated next step is stalled, whatever its stage says.

## Qualification Frameworks

Frameworks are checklists for what you need to know, not scripts. Pick one and use it consistently.

**BANT** — Budget, Authority, Need, Timing. Simple and adequate for smaller, single-buyer sales. Its weakness: budget often does not exist until the need is established, so treat "no budget yet" as a question, not a disqualifier.

**MEDDIC / MEDDPICC** — Metrics, Economic buyer, Decision criteria, Decision process, (Paper process), Identify pain, Champion, (Competition). Built for larger, multi-stakeholder deals. For a small company selling five-figure deals it is usually more than you need, but two of its ideas are valuable at any size:
- **Economic buyer** — the person who can actually approve the spend. If you have never spoken to them, your forecast is a guess.
- **Champion** — someone inside who wants this to happen and will sell it internally when you are not in the room. A friendly contact who has no influence is not a champion.

A minimal version for a founder: before calling a deal qualified, be able to answer — *What problem, in their words? Why now? Who signs? How do they decide and by when? What happens if they do nothing?*

## Pipeline Hygiene

Weekly, in one sitting:

- **Update or kill.** Any deal without buyer activity since the last review gets a concrete next step or is closed lost. Closing a dead deal is information, not failure.
- **Record loss reasons.** Price, timing, no decision, lost to a competitor, bad fit. After a few months the pattern tells you whether to change the product, the price, or who you target.
- **Watch for stage-skipping.** A deal that jumps from lead to negotiation without discovery often stalls later on questions that should have been asked first.
- **Separate "no decision" from "lost."** Deals that die because the buyer never decided usually indicate weak urgency or a missing economic buyer, not a better competitor.

## Coverage — A Heuristic, Not a Law

Pipeline coverage is the value of qualified pipeline divided by the revenue target for the period. Teams often aim for several times the target in qualified pipeline because many deals slip or are lost; the right multiple for you is the inverse of your own historical win rate from the qualified stage. If you win roughly one in three qualified deals, you need about three times your target in qualified pipeline — and only qualified pipeline counts.

Until you have enough closed deals to know your win rate, do not borrow someone else's ratio. Track your own conversion from each stage and use that.

## Signals the Pipeline Is Not Real

- Total pipeline grows every month but closed revenue does not.
- The same deals sit in the same stage for several review cycles.
- Most deals lack a named economic buyer.
- Close dates move forward by a month every month.
- Forecast calls are about hope ("they loved the demo") rather than evidence ("legal has the contract").

When you see these, shrink the pipeline to what meets the exit criteria and plan from that number. A smaller honest pipeline is more useful than a large imaginary one.
