# Running a Bootstrapped Business on Cash

A bootstrapped or self-funded business has one hard constraint: it cannot spend money it does not have. There is no next round to bridge a bad quarter. That makes cash — not revenue, not profit on paper, not valuation — the number that decides survival, and it changes which advice applies. Venture benchmarks (burn multiple, Rule of 40, "raise 18-24 months of runway") assume outside capital is available and the goal is growth at a loss. For a business funded by its customers, the goal is to stay solvent while growing at the pace cash allows.

## Know Your Real Cash Position

Start from the bank balance, then adjust it for money that is not really yours:

- **Subtract tax you owe or will owe** on income already earned (income tax, sales tax or VAT collected, payroll taxes).
- **Subtract committed spend** — contractor invoices, annual subscriptions due soon, deposits you must refund.
- **Do not add receivables yet.** Money owed to you is not cash until it arrives. Track it separately.

What is left is *free cash*: the amount you can actually use to run the business.

## Runway at Current Burn

**Monthly net burn** = cash going out in a month minus cash coming in. For a business with lumpy revenue, use an average over the last three to six months, and also look at the worst recent month.

**Runway (months)** = free cash / monthly net burn.

Run it three ways:
- **Base case** — average revenue and costs.
- **Downside** — revenue at your weakest recent month, costs unchanged.
- **No new sales** — only contracted or recurring revenue continues. This tells you how long you have to fix a problem if sales stall.

If the business is cash-flow positive, runway is not the right question; the questions become how much cash buffer to hold and what to do with the surplus.

Rules of thumb many self-funded operators use: hold a cash buffer of several months of fixed costs (including your own minimum pay) before taking on new commitments, and treat any month where the downside case shows less than a few months of runway as a signal to act on revenue and costs immediately, not later.

## Pay Yourself Deliberately

Owner pay is a real cost, even if you can defer it. Businesses that only look profitable because the founder takes nothing are not yet sustainable.

- Decide a **baseline owner pay** that covers your personal fixed costs, and include it in burn.
- Take anything above that as a **distribution or bonus** from surplus, after the tax set-aside and buffer are funded.
- Keep business and personal accounts separate. Mixing them makes it impossible to see the business's real cash position and complicates tax.

The right legal mechanism (salary, draws, dividends) depends on your entity type and jurisdiction — ask an accountant.

## Set Aside Tax as You Go

Tax is the most common cash surprise for a solo operator. A simple discipline:

- Open a separate account for tax.
- Move a fixed percentage of every payment received into it as it arrives. Ask your accountant what percentage fits your income level, entity and location, and revisit it each year.
- Keep sales tax or VAT you collect in that account too — it was never your money.
- Pay estimated or quarterly taxes on time where they apply, to avoid penalties.

## Receivables and Invoicing Discipline

For service businesses especially, the gap between doing the work and getting paid is where cash crunches come from.

- **Invoice immediately** when a milestone is hit or a month ends — not when you get round to it.
- **Take deposits** before starting work, and bill in milestones rather than at the end.
- **Set clear payment terms** in the contract (due date, late fee if appropriate, what happens to work if invoices go unpaid).
- **Make paying easy** — card or bank transfer links on the invoice.
- **Chase on a schedule.** A polite reminder a few days before the due date, on the due date, and a week after. Escalate to a call after that.
- **Track days to payment by client.** A slow-paying client costs you cash even if their project looks profitable. Price that in, or require deposits.

A receivables list reviewed weekly — who owes what, since when — is as important as the bank balance.

## Pricing Is a Cash Lever

When cash is tight, the quickest lever is often price rather than cost-cutting or volume. Raising prices for new clients, charging for scope that used to be free, moving from hourly billing to fixed or retainer pricing, and asking for upfront payment in exchange for a small discount can all improve cash within weeks. Revenue that arrives earlier is worth more to a bootstrapped business than the same revenue later.

## Cutting Costs Without Cutting Capacity

Review recurring costs quarterly: unused software, overlapping tools, services bought for a growth plan that did not happen. Protect the spending that directly produces revenue or delivery quality; cut what does not.

## When Outside Money Makes Sense

Staying bootstrapped is a choice, not a failure to raise. Outside capital — a loan, revenue-based financing, or equity — can make sense when there is a specific, bounded use with a clear return (for example, financing inventory for confirmed orders, or a hire whose cost is covered by contracted work). Equity investment also changes what the business is for: investors expect growth large enough to return their money, which may not match what the founder wants. Consider raising only if the founder wants that path, and only after the cash fundamentals above are in order.

## A Monthly Cash Routine

1. Reconcile the bank account and update free cash.
2. Update the receivables list and chase anything overdue.
3. Move the tax set-aside and confirm the balance matches what you owe.
4. Recalculate runway (base, downside, no new sales).
5. Pay yourself the baseline; decide whether surplus supports a distribution.
6. Note one action on price, collections or costs for the coming month.
